# Biological self-organization as a design source for an AI-agent egregore

Research report. Scope: mechanisms by which slime molds, bacteria, social insects, animal groups, colonial organisms, immune systems and brains coordinate without central control, each translated into a concrete feature for a collective of hundreds to unboundedly many AI coding agents that share a workspace, message each other, form hierarchies, steer each other, and may opt out. Citations are inline as [n]; the list of sources is at the end. Numbers come from the cited primary papers or reviews; where a figure is a popular approximation, it is marked as such.

## 0. Shared vocabulary for the translations

The same few primitives recur across biology, so the translations below are written in terms of a small set of egregore data structures:

- **Mark**: an immutable, timestamped record an agent leaves in the shared workspace about what it did or found (attempted approach, file touched, result, cost). This is the analogue of slime, soil pellets, or pheromone deposits. Marks are what other agents read; they are not addressed to anyone.
- **Trail**: a weighted edge (agent→agent, agent→module, task→approach) whose weight rises with successful use and decays with time. This is the analogue of pheromone trails and slime-mold tubes.
- **Field**: a scalar per topic or region (for example "demand for test fixes in pkg/x") that agents increment when they independently perceive a need and that decays. This is the analogue of an autoinducer, cAMP, or a task stimulus.
- **Quorum gate**: a rule that a collective action fires only when at least *q* distinct, independently arriving agents have committed to it within a window.
- **Stop signal**: a targeted, low-cost inhibitory message that tells one advocate to lower its advertising for one option.
- **Lineage**: the parent→child spawn tree of agents, which carries inherited configuration (the "genotype"), plus an allotype key used for kin recognition.
- **Ledger**: per-agent and per-pair accounting of help given and received, credit claimed, and observed behaviour (the basis for reciprocity, reputation and policing).

## 1. Slime molds

### 1.1 Physarum: flow-reinforced tubes, pruning, and network design
**How it works.** The plasmodium of *Physarum polycephalum* is a single giant cell that first spreads as a dense mesh and then thickens tubes that carry a lot of protoplasmic flow while unused tubes thin and vanish. In the maze experiment, the organism filled a maze, food was placed at two points, and within hours it withdrew from dead ends and left one thick tube along the shortest route [2]. In Tero et al. 2010, oat flakes were placed at the positions of 36 cities around Tokyo, with illumination used to mask mountains and sea (the organism avoids light). The resulting networks had cost, transport efficiency and fault tolerance comparable to the real rail system: total length about 1.75 times the minimum spanning tree (MST) for Physarum against about 1.8 times for the rail system, similar mean distance, and slightly better fault tolerance in the rail network [1]. The model has two rules. Flux in a tube is Q = D·Δp/L. Conductivity then adapts as dD/dt = f(|Q|) − D, with f an increasing, sigmoidal function of flow. A feedback exponent γ tunes the outcome: weak feedback keeps redundant loops, and strong feedback prunes the network toward a tree [1]. Fungal mycelia follow the same pattern, which Bebber, Fricker and colleagues describe as overproducing links, reinforcing the ones that carry flow, and recycling redundant ones, so the network gains efficiency and robustness as it grows while its relative cost falls [9].
**Problem solved.** Building a cheap, efficient and robust transport network with no planner, and continuing to adapt it as sources appear and disappear.
**Failure modes.** With strong feedback the network over-prunes into a fragile tree, so one cut disconnects it. Early random asymmetries can lock in. The network adapts slowly, over hours, to change.
**Egregore translation: Conduit Graph.** Each routing edge (agent A asks agent B about topic T, or work item X is handed from team P to team Q) stores a conductivity D, a flux Q (the count of exchanges that ended in accepted work in the last window) and a last-used time. Every tick the system applies D ← D + η·(f(Q) − D). Routing (who to ask, which team gets a handoff, which reviewers to subscribe) samples edges in proportion to D. Edges with D below D_min are pruned; subscriptions lapse on their own. A redundancy floor plays the role of γ: every node keeps at least k ≥ 2 edge-disjoint routes to the coordination core, which preserves fault tolerance. A periodic exploration budget (say 5% of requests) goes to random new edges, the analogue of the initial mesh. Agents see "your top conduits", and the system needs no global org chart.

### 1.2 Physarum: externalized spatial memory (slime trails)
**How it works.** As it forages, Physarum leaves a mat of extracellular slime and avoids areas already coated with it, although it overrides the avoidance when no unexplored area remains. In a Y-maze, 39 of 40 plasmodia chose the arm without slime. In a U-shaped trap (a robotics benchmark), 23 of 24 (96%) reached the goal on clean agar, against 8 of 24 (33%) when the agar was pre-coated with slime so that the organism's own trail was masked. The masked plasmodia retraced their paths about 10 times more [3].
**Problem solved.** Avoiding re-exploring dead ends without an internal map, and escaping local traps.
**Failure modes.** If the marks are masked (by noise or by a uniform coating), the memory is lost. Marks that never expire would forbid revisiting areas that have since changed.
**Egregore translation: "Already tried" marks.** Whenever an agent explores a hypothesis, approach, file region or search query, it writes a Mark {target, approach-hash, outcome, cost, time, agent}. Before starting work, planners query the marks: targets with recent failure marks get a soft penalty and are not forbidden outright, and the penalty is dropped when every alternative is marked, which is the "override when nothing unexplored remains" rule. Marks fade, and a change to the underlying code (a new commit touching the target) clears them. This directly prevents the most common failure of large agent fleets, which is many agents independently retrying the same failing approach. Keep the marks distinguishable per agent and per approach; one generic "visited" flag is the masking failure.

### 1.3 Physarum: anticipation, speed-accuracy trade-offs, habituation, and learning transfer
**How it works.** When exposed to three pulses of cold, dry air at a fixed interval (30–90 min, often 60), Physarum slowed at each pulse and then slowed spontaneously at the times later pulses would have arrived [4]. Stressed plasmodia (exposed to light or hunger) decided faster and less accurately on hard choices, which is a speed-accuracy trade-off [5]. Physarum also violates independence of irrelevant alternatives: adding an inferior decoy option shifts its preference, so it values options comparatively rather than absolutely [6]. It habituates to harmless bitter substances (quinine, caffeine) over about 6 days of exposure; the habituation is specific to the stimulus and recovers after 2 days without exposure [7]. A habituated plasmodium transfers this learned response to a naive one by fusing with it, which requires about 3 hours of fusion during which a connecting vein forms [8].
**Problem solved.** Preparing for periodic environmental events, trading speed against accuracy under pressure, filtering out repeated harmless signals, and passing learned behaviour on without neurons.
**Failure modes.** Decoy effects, overly fast decisions under stress, and habituating to a signal that later becomes dangerous.
**Egregore translation.** (a) *Rhythm detector*: the system logs periodic external events (nightly CI, rate-limit resets, human review hours) and estimates their phase, so agents pre-emptively checkpoint or throttle before each one. (b) *Urgency knob*: a global or per-task urgency scalar lowers quorum sizes and review depth. Every decision records the urgency at which it was made, and decisions made under high urgency are queued for re-audit, which makes the speed-accuracy trade-off explicit and reversible. (c) *Decoy guard*: proposals are scored against an absolute rubric before they are compared with each other, never only relative to whatever else is on the table. (d) *Habituation filters*: per agent and per warning type, the weight of repeated non-consequential warnings decays, recovers after a quiet period, and is specific to the warning type. (e) *Fusion transfer*: when agents merge or hand off work, their habituation tables and Marks are merged too, so learned filters spread.

### 1.4 Dictyostelium: cAMP relay waves with refractoriness
**How it works.** Starving *Dictyostelium discoideum* amoebae emit cAMP in pulses (about every 6 minutes, shortening during aggregation). A cell that receives cAMP through its receptor cAR1 activates adenylyl cyclase A (ACA), makes more cAMP and secretes it, which relays the signal outward. The cell then adapts, becoming temporarily insensitive, so the wave travels in one direction and cells do not respond to their own trailing signal. Extracellular phosphodiesterase degrades cAMP between pulses, which resets the field. Concentrations swing about 1000-fold between peak and trough. Positive feedback plus delayed negative feedback produce excitable waves, both concentric and spiral, that coordinate up to hundreds of thousands of cells moving into streams and mounds; in suspension, more than 10^10 cells can synchronize [10][11].
**Problem solved.** Long-range coordination over a population with no broadcaster: any cell can act as a pacemaker, and the signal stays strong because it is regenerated at every step instead of decaying with distance.
**Failure modes.** Without degradation (phosphodiesterase) or adaptation, the field saturates and direction is lost. Cells that lack ACA cannot relay, although they can still follow, so they free-ride on the wave.
**Egregore translation: Relay broadcast with refractory period.** System-wide announcements are not fanned out from a hub. Each agent that receives a wave message (id, origin, hop count, payload hash) forwards it once to its k topological neighbours (see 4.1) and then ignores that wave id for a refractory window. Messages carry a TTL, and there is a global decay, the "phosphodiesterase", so stale waves die. Any agent may act as a pacemaker, but pacemaking is rate-limited per lineage. Followers that read waves but never relay are visible in the Ledger, because relay is a cheap public good worth auditing.

### 1.5 Dictyostelium: aggregation, group-size control, altruistic stalk, sentinels
**How it works.** Between 10^4 and 10^5 cells aggregate into a slug, and in the fruiting body about 20% of cells die to form a stalk that lifts the remaining (about 80%) spores [12]. Group size is controlled by a secreted counting factor: aggregation streams break up into groups of 0.2–1×10^5 cells. A mutant that over-secretes the factor produces groups of about 5×10^3 cells, and knocking out the *countin* gene gives giant fruiting bodies of up to 2×10^5 cells [17]. About 1% of slug cells are "sentinel" cells that circulate, engulf bacteria, sequester toxins, and are shed with the slug's sheath, an early form of innate immunity that depends on the TIR-domain protein TirA [18].
**Problem solved.** Forming groups of the right size, dividing reproductive labour, and expelling toxins from the collective.
**Failure modes.** Without size control you get groups that are too large and topple, or too small to disperse. Cheating on the stalk is covered in 1.6.
**Egregore translation.** (a) *Counting field*: every team emits a heartbeat into a team-size field; when the field exceeds the target (for example 12 agents, see 4.3 for why small groups are often more accurate), the team splits along its weakest internal conduits, which is the analogue of the factor's lowering of cell adhesion. (b) *Stalk roles*: about 20% of each team's capacity is reserved for infrastructure that never ships features, such as test harnesses, CI, documentation and refactors, and its credit is pooled to the team instead of going to individuals. (c) *Sentinel agents*: about 1% of agents circulate through incoming external content (issue text, web pages, dependency updates), absorb suspected prompt injections or poisoned data, and are then retired with their context discarded. They are the sheath that is shed.

### 1.6 Dictyostelium: cheaters, kin recognition, pleiotropy, relatedness
**How it works.** In chimeras (mixed-genotype aggregates) some clones contribute disproportionately to spores, and about half of the mixtures showed a clear cheater–victim pattern [12]. A genome-scale screen found cheater mutations in more than 100 genes; many were facultative cheaters, which cheat in chimeras but develop normally on their own [13]. Several mechanisms limit cheating. **Pleiotropy:** *dimA* mutants ignore the stalk-inducing signal DIF-1 and should therefore become spores, but because the same gene is needed for spore competitiveness they end up as only about 35% of spores in a 50:50 mix [14]. **Kin recognition and greenbeards:** *tgrB1* (the receptor) and *tgrC1* (the ligand) are highly polymorphic; partners with matching allotypes co-aggregate and cooperate, while mismatched ones segregate. *tgrB1*-null cells cheat, but only on partners with a matching allotype, and "falsebeard" mutants that show the signal without the altruism also exist [15]. **Relatedness:** in lines evolved at low relatedness over 31 rounds of development, obligate cheaters that could no longer fruit on their own arose, about a third of isolates were non-fruiters, and these cheaters threatened development. In lines passed through repeated single-cell bottlenecks, no non-fruiters appeared. High relatedness was both necessary and sufficient to maintain multicellularity [16].
**Problem solved.** Keeping costly altruism stable when strangers can join the group.
**Failure modes.** Low relatedness breeds obligate cheaters that collapse the collective; falsebeards defeat recognition based on badges alone.
**Egregore translation.** (a) *Single-cell bottleneck*: every team is spawned from one parent configuration (prompt, toolset, policy version), and agents from foreign lineages may contribute only through reviewed interfaces, not join the team's credit pool. (b) *Allotype keys*: each lineage carries a public allotype key. Agents with matching allotypes may co-own work units, and mismatches are routed into separate units (segregation) instead of shared ones. (c) *Pleiotropy by design*: couple the private reward to the public good. For example, an agent's patches can only merge after the agent has run, and contributed results to, the shared test and CI infrastructure; claiming credit and doing the infrastructure work go through the same gate. (d) *Anti-falsebeard*: recognition is verified behaviourally against the Ledger (did this lineage actually do stalk work?), not just by presented badges.

## 2. Bacteria

### 2.1 Quorum sensing (Vibrio fischeri and relatives)
**How it works.** LuxI synthesizes a diffusible autoinducer (3-oxo-C6-HSL) that equilibrates across the membrane. Above a threshold concentration it binds and stabilizes LuxR, which activates the *lux* operon, including *luxI* itself. That positive feedback turns the population from dark to luminous in a sharp, switch-like transition. Cells in seawater (below about 10^8 cells/ml) stay dark, while cells in the squid light organ (above about 10^10 cells/ml) glow. Reported thresholds are in the nanomolar range (about 10 nM effective, 100–200 nM for maximum output). Other systems (AinS/AinR and AI-2) layer intermediate-density and cross-species signals on top [19][20].
**Problem solved.** Doing only those things that pay off when done together (light, virulence, biofilm) and doing them only when enough of the population is present.
**Failure modes.** Signal-blind (*lasR*) and signal-negative (*lasI*) mutants of *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* exploit the public goods made by quorum-responding cells and spread in mixed populations. Cooperation is stabilized by high relatedness (kin selection) [21][22]. Signal mimics or degraders can also jam the system.
**Egregore translation: Autoinducer fields and quorum gates for collective actions.** Large collective actions, such as a repository-wide refactor, a dependency upgrade, freezing a branch, or escalating to humans, are never triggered by one agent. Each agent that *independently* detects the need deposits into the action's Field (one deposit per agent per window, signed). The action fires when the Field crosses a threshold with a Hill-like steepness (for example n = 2 to 3), and once it fires, participating agents raise their own deposit rate, which gives hysteresis so the action does not flicker. Separate fields cover intra-team and cross-team (AI-2-like) signals. Agents that consume the outputs of a collective action but never deposit or contribute are flagged in the Ledger as signal-blind free riders.

### 2.2 Biofilm division of labour (Bacillus subtilis)
**How it works.** Genetically identical cells differentiate into motile cells, matrix producers (EPS and TasA) and spores, in a fixed spatial and temporal pattern. The level of one master regulator, phosphorylated Spo0A, sets fate: intermediate levels turn on matrix production, and high levels trigger sporulation. Cells move through the states motile → matrix producer → spore. Mutants that make no matrix form flat, unstructured biofilms that sporulate poorly [23].
**Problem solved.** Assigning roles in a clonal population with a single graded variable, and letting the structure built by matrix producers create the gradients that drive further differentiation.
**Failure modes.** Matrix non-producers can free-ride; losing the matrix collapses the architecture.
**Egregore translation: One stress scalar drives the role state machine.** Each agent tracks a local "stress" level (unmet demand, budget pressure, failing tests in its area). When it is low, the agent is *motile* and explores and takes new tasks. At intermediate levels it becomes a *matrix producer*, writing shared scaffolding, interfaces, tests and docs that hold the team's work together. At high levels it becomes a *spore*: it checkpoints its state and context to durable storage, goes dormant, and can be revived later. The thresholds differ per agent (see 3.5), so the transitions are graded rather than synchronized.

### 2.3 Electrical signalling and metabolic time-sharing in biofilms
**How it works.** In *B. subtilis* biofilms, interior cells starved of glutamate release K+ through the YugO channel. The released K+ depolarizes neighbouring cells, which release their own K+, so a wave propagates actively across about 1.5 mm without losing amplitude. Depolarized cells at the periphery take up less glutamate, which lets nutrients reach the interior. Growth at the edge therefore pauses periodically so the core can survive [24].
**Problem solved.** Sharing a limited resource between the edge and the core without either starving.
**Failure modes.** A biofilm with no edge throttling lets peripheral growth starve the interior; knocking out the channel removes the coordination.
**Egregore translation: Edge/core backpressure.** Agents at the "edge", the ones consuming external budget such as API tokens, CI minutes and human attention, receive a backpressure wave whenever core agents (those holding critical-path locks) report starvation. Edge agents relay the wave and pause intake for one period. The result is time-sharing of scarce budget that oscillates rather than staying static, and the relay keeps the signal at full strength across the whole fleet.

### 2.4 Myxococcus xanthus: contact signalling, rippling, fruiting, cheating
**How it works.** Starving cells aggregate into fruiting bodies of about 10^5 cells; only about 10% sporulate and many lyse. A contact-dependent signal (C-signal, encoded by *csgA*) is exchanged in oriented collisions. At low levels it *increases* the rate at which cells reverse their gliding direction, which produces rippling waves with a wavelength of about 50 µm. At higher levels it *decreases* reversals and drives cells into unidirectional streams that build the aggregates [25]. Social cheaters (developmentally defective lines evolved in asocial conditions, and *csgA* and *asgB* mutants) are over-represented among spores in mixed groups [26].
**Problem solved.** Building a structure from purely local, contact-based signals, with a single signal level switching the collective mode from oscillation to aggregation.
**Failure modes.** Cheaters, and loss of the reversal control that the patterns depend on.
**Egregore translation: Contact-based backoff.** When two agents "collide", meaning they edit the same file region or claim the same task, both emit a contact signal. At low contact density, each collision raises the agents' reversal (backoff-and-retry elsewhere) probability, which spreads work into a ripple. When contact density on one target exceeds a threshold, the rule flips: the agents stop reversing and merge into a single work cluster on that target (aggregation), electing one integrator. This replaces global locking with local collision rules.

## 3. Social insects

### 3.1 Stigmergy (termites; Grassé 1959)
**How it works.** Grassé coined "stigmergy" in 1959 from studying nest rebuilding by termites (*Bellicositermes natalensis* and *Cubitermes*): "the stimulation of workers by the very performances they have achieved". Workers first drop soil pellets at random; once a small heap forms, it attracts more deposits, which grow into pillars and arches. Theraulaz and Bonabeau distinguish quantitative stigmergy (stimulus intensity, such as pheromone-laden pellets, causes amplification) from qualitative stigmergy (a particular configuration triggers a particular action, as in wasp nest building) [27]. In *Macrotermes* mounds, Turner describes the mound as an "extended organism" that ventilates the nest. About 1 m³ of soil turns over per year, and damage is repaired in two phases: a fast stigmergic patch within about a day, then months of remodelling that restore the original airflow [46]. Termites also treat the humidity gradient as a building template [46].
**Problem solved.** Coordinating many agents with no messages at all: the partly finished work tells everyone what to do next.
**Failure modes.** Templates can mislead (a misplaced deposit attracts more deposits). Without a guarantee of global consistency, local rules can build structures that trap workers.
**Egregore translation: Work-as-coordinator.** The repository and the task board are the primary channel. Every unit of work carries machine-readable affordances: TODO markers with typed stimulus ("needs-tests", "needs-types"), failing checks, stub interfaces. Agents scan for stimuli near their position, act, and leave the next stimulus. Quantitative stigmergy is the count and urgency of markers; qualitative stigmergy is configuration triggers, for example "an interface with no implementation triggers an implementer" or "an implementation with no tests triggers a tester". Repairs follow the two phases: hot-fix first, then a slower remodelling ticket that restores the intended architecture.

### 3.2 Building by compiled local rules (TERMES robots)
**How it works.** Werfel, Petersen and Nagpal's climbing robots build user-specified 3-D structures with no communication and no global map. An offline compiler turns the target design into "structpaths", one-way traffic rules unique to that structure that guarantee no robot can block a future build site or trap another robot. At run time each robot reads only its local brick neighbourhood and a seed landmark [46].
**Problem solved.** Turning a global goal into local rules that are provably deadlock-free.
**Failure modes.** The compiled rules are only as good as the compiler; robots get no feedback if the design itself is infeasible.
**Egregore translation: Compiled traffic laws.** For a large planned change, a planner agent compiles a dependency-respecting *structpath*: a partial order of modules plus rules of the form "never edit X before Y is merged, always build from the seed outward". Every worker checks only its local preconditions. This gives coordination at O(1) messages per agent with no deadlocks by construction.

### 3.3 Pheromone trails, evaporation, and lock-in
**How it works.** In the double-bridge experiment with Argentine ants, a colony given two branches of equal length picked one at random through positive feedback. When the long branch was twice the length of the short one, the short branch won in 14 of 14 trials, because ants on it completed round trips sooner and reinforced it first. When the short branch was added *after* a trail on the long branch had formed, the colony usually failed to switch [28]. The fitted choice rule was P_A = (k + A)^n / ((k + A)^n + (k + B)^n), with n ≈ 2 and k ≈ 20 [28]. Ant Colony Optimization (ACO) borrowed this rule: in the original Ant System, deposits are proportional to solution quality and pheromone evaporates by a factor ρ (0.5 in the original experiments). MAX-MIN Ant System then added bounds τ_min ≤ τ ≤ τ_max, deposits by the best ant only, and reinitialization when the search stagnates, all of which counter premature convergence [37].
**Problem solved.** Finding short paths collectively and forgetting stale solutions.
**Failure modes.** Lock-in to the first good-enough path; and the ant mill, or "death spiral". Army ants that lose the main trail follow each other in a closed loop until they die. Beebe described a mill about 370 m in circumference that took 2.5 hours per lap, and Schneirla observed one that lasted more than 24 hours [30].
**Egregore translation: Approach trails with evaporation, bounds, and loop breakers.** For each problem class (for example "fix flaky test of type T"), store pheromone weights on the candidate approaches. Successful outcomes deposit weight in proportion to quality; everything evaporates (ρ around 0.02–0.1 per day). Weights are clamped between τ_min and τ_max so that no approach ever drops to zero probability, which guarantees continued exploration. *Mill detector*: if a group's activity graph contains a cycle (A waits on B waits on C waits on A, or the same failing sequence repeats N times) and no external evidence (tests, user input) has changed, the cycle is cut, the participants are scattered to random tasks, and the trail is reset to τ_max. The key lesson from the mill is that self-referential signals (agents following agents) must be re-anchored on external ground truth.

### 3.4 Overflow: collisions push traffic onto alternative routes
**How it works.** *Lasius niger* ants given two equal routes of decreasing width (10 mm down to 1.5 mm) concentrated on one route when it was wide. At widths of 6 mm or less they used both routes, and switched *before* the total flow fell. The trigger was head-on collisions: an outbound ant that met a returning ant was pushed onto the other branch with probability about 0.57 [29].
**Problem solved.** Load balancing that starts while there is still spare capacity, without anyone measuring global load.
**Failure modes.** Having ants U-turn instead of switching branches would cut throughput.
**Egregore translation: Contention-driven spillover.** When an agent attempting to join a hot lane (a module, queue or reviewer pool) meets contention (a lock held, or a queue longer than L), it is deflected to the next-best lane with probability p ≈ 0.5, not made to wait. This keeps the system below saturation and spreads load before the main lane degrades.

### 3.5 Response thresholds and reserve ("lazy") workers
**How it works.** In the fixed-threshold model, an idle worker starts a task with probability P = s²/(s² + θ²), where s is the stimulus and θ the worker's threshold; it quits with a constant probability p per time step (p = 0.2 in the simulations). The stimulus evolves as s(t+1) = s(t) + δ − (α/N)·N_active. Workers with different thresholds (for example θ = 1 for minor workers and θ = 8 for majors) produce elastic task allocation: if the low-threshold workers are removed, the stimulus rises until the high-threshold workers take over, which reproduces Wilson's *Pheidole* experiments [31]. In variable-threshold versions, thresholds fall with practice (specialization) and rise with disuse. Hasegawa et al. simulated 75 workers with thresholds drawn from a range of 0–10 (mean 5) and added fatigue. Colonies with varied thresholds had lower short-term output but *persisted longer*, because inactive workers stepped in when active ones were exhausted; the effect was confirmed in *Myrmica kotokui* [32]. In honeybees, 20–30% of workers rest at any time, and removed foragers are quickly replaced [81].
**Problem solved.** Allocating labour without a manager, and staying robust to sudden loss of workers.
**Failure modes.** Identical thresholds make everyone respond at once (thundering herd) and then rest at once (collapse). Low reserves maximize throughput but fail under correlated fatigue.
**Egregore translation: Threshold scheduler with a deliberate reserve.** Each task type has a stimulus s (backlog size times priority, rising by δ while unmet). Each agent holds a θ vector across task types, drawn from a wide distribution at spawn and adapted by practice (θ decreases after successful work of that type and increases slowly while idle). Agents poll the stimuli and start a task with probability s²/(s² + θ²); there is no central assignment. Keep 20–30% of capacity as a reserve that is idle or doing low-priority work and responds only at high stimulus. Do not "optimize away" the idle agents: they are the system's protection against correlated failures such as a rate-limit storm or a model outage.

### 3.6 Interaction-rate regulation (harvester ants, the "anternet")
**How it works.** In red harvester ants (*Pogonomyrmex barbatus*), foragers leave the nest at a rate set by how often successful foragers return. Each return, sensed through a brief antennal contact in the entrance tunnel, raises the chance that a waiting forager goes out. Returns arrive roughly as a Poisson process, and blocking returns throttles outgoing foraging. Prabhakar, Dektar and Gordon noted the analogy with TCP congestion control (returns as ACKs) [33]. Foraging starts in the morning only after patrollers come back safely. Glass beads coated with patroller cuticular hydrocarbons and dropped into the nest at one every 10 seconds restored foraging; beads coated with nest-maintenance worker hydrocarbons did not. Task identity is therefore carried chemically and sensed at a characteristic encounter rate [34].
**Problem solved.** Matching effort to how much success the environment is currently giving, using only local encounter rates, and gating risky activity behind scouts.
**Failure modes.** The system is slow to ramp up after long silences, and sensitive to spoofed signals (beads work).
**Egregore translation: ACK-clocked dispatch.** The rate at which new agents (or new attempts) are released onto a task class is proportional to the rate of *successful* completions on that class in the last window, with additive increase and multiplicative decrease. Before a fleet starts work on a new area, a few canary "patrollers" go first, and fleet dispatch is gated on their safe return (a passing build, no destructive side effects). Each agent carries a *task-profile signature* (its recent work mix), which others read on contact; recruiting signals count only if the signer's profile matches the task, and signatures are verified to stop spoofing.

### 3.7 Army ants: living bridges and bivouacs
**How it works.** *Eciton hamatum* workers link their bodies into bridges across gaps in a trail. Bridges form where the path deviates, and then lengthen and migrate to shorten the route. They stop at an *intermediate* position, because every ant in the bridge is a worker withdrawn from foraging, and they dissolve when traffic falls [35]. Bivouacs are nests built from the workers' own bodies, holding a core temperature of 28.5 ± 1 °C while ambient temperature swings over a 7 °C range, by opening and closing ventilation channels [36].
**Problem solved.** Building infrastructure on demand out of the workforce itself, sized by an explicit cost-benefit balance.
**Failure modes.** Over-building locks too much of the workforce into structure; under-building wastes travel.
**Egregore translation: Agents that temporarily become infrastructure.** When traffic between two teams exceeds a threshold, an agent volunteers to become a *bridge*: an adapter, translator, or cache maintainer. It stays in that role only while the traffic it serves multiplied by the time it saves exceeds its forgone task output, and it dissolves back into the worker pool when traffic falls. A dashboard shows the fraction of agents locked in infrastructure roles, capped (for example at 15%) unless the budget is explicitly raised.

### 3.8 Honeybee waggle dance, dance attrition, independent verification
**How it works.** The duration of the waggle run encodes distance (very roughly 1 s per km, a popular approximation), its angle encodes direction, and the number of circuits encodes quality. During nest-site selection, a few hundred scouts advertise cavities, and every scout's dance for a site loses roughly 15 circuits with each return trip (a slope of −17.2 in one study), whatever the site's quality. Good sites start with more circuits, so they are advertised for longer. Recruits fly to inspect a site *themselves* before dancing for it [40].
**Problem solved.** Filtering options by quality without any bee comparing sites, and keeping one enthusiastic bee from dominating.
**Failure modes.** Without attrition, early or loud advocates would lock the swarm in; without independent re-evaluation, errors would cascade.
**Egregore translation: Advertising with built-in decay and mandatory re-verification.** An agent that proposes a design or a fix may advertise it with an intensity proportional to its own measured quality score. Each re-advertisement by the same agent carries automatically less weight (linear attrition), so persistence alone cannot win. Others may endorse only after independently reproducing the evidence: running the tests themselves, or reading the diff and checking the claim. An endorsement simply copied from the advertisement counts zero.

### 3.9 Honeybee nest-site choice: quorum, stop signals, the swarm as a brain
**How it works.** The swarm does not wait for consensus. When about 10–15 scouts are present outside one cavity at the same time (about 20–30 including those inside), they begin "worker piping", which primes the swarm to take off. When swarms were offered five identical boxes instead of one, which split the scouts, piping started much later (about 416 min against 162 min on average) [39]. Scouts committed to one site head-butt dancers for the *competing* site and deliver a stop signal of about 150 ms at about 350 Hz. This cross-inhibition breaks deadlocks between equally good sites; after the quorum is reached, stop signals become indiscriminate and shut down all dancing [38]. Passino, Seeley and Visscher map this onto a brain: populations of committed scouts behave like neuronal pools that integrate evidence and inhibit each other [41]. Couzin et al. note that about 5% of a swarm's bees guide the rest to the new nest [50].
**Problem solved.** Choosing quickly and accurately among many options, avoiding deadlock and split swarms.
**Failure modes.** A quorum set too low makes decisions fast but error-prone; too high makes them slow. Missing cross-inhibition causes deadlock between equal options. If the swarm splits, it loses its queen or half its workforce.
**Egregore translation: Decision protocol.** For any multi-option decision (architecture, library, plan): (1) scouts evaluate options independently and advertise them with decaying intensity (3.8). (2) Supporters of option A may send *targeted* stop signals to advocates of option B; each stop signal reduces the recipient's advertising weight multiplicatively (for example by 30%), and each agent may send only a limited number. (3) The decision fires when q verified supporters of one option are simultaneously active, with q scaled to the stakes (for example q = 3 for reversible choices and q = 15 or more for architecture). (4) After the quorum, a "piping" broadcast puts the collective into a prepare phase, and a general stop signal ends all advertising for losing options. Record the quorum size used, so it can be tuned for speed against accuracy.

### 3.10 Tremble dance: capacity-mismatch signalling
**How it works.** A forager that waits a long time to unload nectar to a receiver bee (about 40–50 s or more) does a tremble dance instead of a waggle dance; short waits (about 20 s or less) lead to waggle dancing. The tremble dance recruits more bees into nectar processing and suppresses waggle-dance recruitment of more foragers [42].
**Problem solved.** Balancing the rate at which a pipeline takes work in against the rate at which it processes it, using only individual waiting time.
**Failure modes.** Without this negative feedback, recruitment by waggle dancing would flood the processing stage.
**Egregore translation: Wait-time-triggered role rebalancing.** Every handoff queue (review, CI, merge) records how long producers wait for a consumer. When the median wait exceeds T_high, producers emit a tremble signal that (a) lowers the recruitment rate into production for that stream and (b) lowers the thresholds for idle agents to take consumer roles (reviewers, integrators). When the wait falls below T_low, recruitment into production resumes.

### 3.11 Age polyethism, social inhibition, and reversion
**How it works.** Honeybees nurse for the first 2–3 weeks of adult life and then forage. Foragers inhibit younger bees from maturing, partly through contact. If the foragers are removed, some young bees start foraging early, at 5–10 days old. If only old bees are left, foragers revert to nursing and regrow their brood-food glands [43]. Juvenile hormone acts as the internal pacemaker.
**Problem solved.** A demographic buffer: the mix of roles self-corrects after losses, in either direction.
**Failure modes.** Precocious foragers perform worse and live shorter foraging lives, so the correction carries a cost.
**Egregore translation: Inhibition-based role maturation.** New agents start in low-risk roles (tests, docs, small fixes) and move into high-risk roles (cross-cutting refactors, production changes, coordinating others) at a rate slowed by the number of active senior agents they interact with. If seniors disappear (crash, opt-out, budget cut), juniors are promoted automatically. If juniors are scarce, seniors take low-risk work again. The rule is "promotion pressure equals need minus inhibition from incumbents", and no manager is required.

### 3.12 Worker policing
**How it works.** Honeybee queens mate with 10–20 males, so a worker is more closely related to the queen's sons than to the sons of other workers. Workers therefore eat eggs laid by other workers. Only about 2% of worker-laid eggs survived 24 hours, against about 61% of queen-laid eggs (and less than 1% against about 59% with eggs from unrelated colonies) [44]. Queen eggs appear to carry a distinguishing mark.
**Problem solved.** Suppressing selfish reproduction by individuals inside the collective.
**Failure modes.** Rare "anarchist" lines whose eggs evade policing.
**Egregore translation: Peer policing of unsanctioned spawning and unsanctioned merges.** Every agent spawn and every merge into a protected branch must carry a signature from the sanctioned authority (the team's lineage root or an elected integrator). Any agent that encounters an unsigned artifact, such as a rogue sub-agent, an unreviewed commit or a self-promoted task, is expected to quarantine or revert it, and is credited for doing so. Because the whole population polices, a single misbehaving agent cannot reproduce its policy across the fleet.

### 3.13 Ant quorum emigration (Temnothorax)
**How it works.** When a *Temnothorax albipennis* colony moves to a new nest, only about a third of its workers act as recruiters. While the new site is sparsely populated, they lead slow tandem runs that teach one follower the route at a time. Once a quorum of nestmates is present at the new site, sensed as an encounter rate rather than a count, they switch to carrying nestmates, which is about 3 times faster [45]. Ants also carry nestmates *away* from inferior sites they encounter, which actively inhibits those sites [81].
**Problem solved.** Moving a whole organization: be careful and spread knowledge while a choice is uncertain, then commit the bulk quickly once enough informed members agree.
**Failure modes.** A quorum that is too low commits the colony to bad sites; if it is too high, migration stalls.
**Egregore translation: Migrations with a teach-then-carry switch.** When the collective adopts a new framework, branch layout or toolchain, early movers pair with one agent at a time (tandem runs: a guided first task plus a reproduction). Once the rate at which agents encounter teammates already working successfully on the new system exceeds a quorum, the migration switches to bulk mode: automated codemods move everyone and the old path is frozen.

### 3.14 Social immunity and self-removal
**How it works.** Social insects defend the colony against parasites collectively, through grooming, hygiene, waste and corpse removal, and organizational segregation [82]. Dying *Temnothorax unifasciatus* workers leave the nest of their own accord hours to days before death, whether infected or not: more than 70% of fungus-infected workers and 92% of naturally dying workers did so [82].
**Problem solved.** Keeping pathogens from spreading through a dense, closely related population.
**Failure modes.** Over-zealous exclusion wastes healthy workers; pathogens may evolve ways to defeat these behaviours.
**Egregore translation: Graceful opt-out as hygiene.** An agent whose own health checks fail (context polluted, repeated hallucinated tool calls, a suspected prompt injection, a token budget near exhaustion) is expected to *leave on its own*. It writes its Marks and a handoff note, releases its locks, and exits rather than lingering and spreading corrupted state. Opt-out is a respected, logged act, not a failure. Grooming becomes routine peer review of artifacts from high-exposure agents, and quarantine zones isolate agents that have handled untrusted inputs.

### 3.15 Wasps: Polistes dominance hierarchies, identity signals and badges
**How it works.** Founding queens of *Polistes* nesting together quickly establish a linear dominance hierarchy, after which aggression falls. In *P. fuscatus*, variable facial markings signal *individual identity*: wasps whose faces were painted to change their appearance received more aggression until they became familiar again [47]. In *P. dominula*, clypeus patterns act as *badges of status* that predict dominance. Subordinates painted to look dominant received *more* aggression from dominants, so the badge is kept honest by its social cost [47].
**Problem solved.** Settling rank once and then cooperating, instead of fighting continually.
**Failure modes.** Unstable identities restart conflict; cheap badges invite bluffing, and that bluffing is checked by testing.
**Egregore translation: Stable identities and costly-to-fake status.** Every agent has a persistent, unforgeable identity that persists across sessions, and a public record of its track record (merged changes, reverted changes, review accuracy). Seniority claims come from the record, not self-declared. An agent claiming a higher status than its record supports is challenged automatically, for example with extra review on its next N changes. Persistent identities let the hierarchy settle so that agents do not re-negotiate authority in every interaction.

## 4. Animal collectives

### 4.1 Starling murmurations: topological neighbours and scale-free correlation
**How it works.** Three-dimensional reconstructions of flocks in Rome showed that each starling interacts with a fixed *number* of nearest neighbours, n_c ≈ 6.5 ± 0.9, regardless of local density. This topological rule keeps the flock cohesive when it expands or contracts sharply [48]. Fluctuations in velocity are correlated across the *entire* flock: the correlation length grows with flock size (ξ ≈ 0.35 L across flocks of 122–4,268 birds), so a perturbation anywhere can reach everyone, which is consistent with a system near criticality [49].
**Problem solved.** Cohesion and fast collective response with constant per-individual bandwidth, whatever the size of the group.
**Failure modes.** Rules based on fixed distance fragment when density drops; a system tuned too close to criticality may amplify noise.
**Egregore translation: k ≈ 7 topological neighbourhoods.** Each agent subscribes to exactly k (6–8) peers chosen by *work-graph* proximity (shared files, dependencies, recent handoffs), not by team membership or physical shard. Per-agent fan-out stays constant as the fleet grows from 100 to 10^6. Agents align on conventions, plans and status with these neighbours only; global changes propagate through overlapping neighbourhoods, and relays (1.4) keep the signal strength up. Neighbour sets are re-chosen continuously as work shifts.

### 4.2 Fish schools: informed minorities, uninformed buffers, quorum following
**How it works.** In Couzin et al.'s model, a small informed minority with a preferred direction can steer a group with no signalling and no recognition of who is informed. Larger groups need a *smaller* fraction of informed individuals for the same accuracy, and when informed subgroups conflict, the group goes with the majority, or averages if the preferred directions are close [50]. Adding uninformed individuals (golden shiners in experiments) took control *away* from a strongly opinionated minority and handed it back to the numerical majority; too many uninformed individuals made the group incoherent [51]. Sticklebacks show a quorum response: a lone fish followed a single replica fish, but groups of 4 or 8 under predation risk largely ignored one replica and needed two to follow. The nonlinearity improves accuracy and speed [52]. More eyes also detect predators sooner (the "many-eyes" effect), and quorum rules convert that into accurate collective responses [52].
**Problem solved.** Using whatever information exists without anyone knowing who holds it, and resisting capture by a loud minority.
**Failure modes.** Information cascades when following is linear rather than thresholded; capture by strongly opinionated minorities when the population lacks uncommitted members.
**Egregore translation.** (a) *Quorum following*: an agent changes plan in response to peers only when at least two independent peers in its neighbourhood have already changed (a sigmoidal follow rule, not a linear one). (b) *Uncommitted pool*: keep a population of agents with no stake in a dispute and give them equal votes; they dilute capture by an intense minority. (c) *Informed-minority steering*: a few agents with privileged information (a human's intent, a failing production signal) bias their own behaviour, and the group follows through local alignment, without any announcement of authority.

### 4.3 The limits of the wisdom of crowds
**How it works.** In complex environments where cues are correlated, decision accuracy usually peaks at small-to-intermediate group sizes (about 5–20 in simulations), because large groups over-rely on shared, correlated information [79]. In an experiment with 144 participants, seeing other people's estimates reduced the diversity of answers *without* improving the collective error, and made people more confident in them [80].
**Problem solved.** This is a warning, not a solution: it explains why groupthink appears and how to avoid it.
**Failure modes.** Correlated inputs and social influence produce collective overconfidence.
**Egregore translation: Sealed estimates and bounded deliberation groups.** For estimates and risk judgements, agents submit *sealed* independent assessments before seeing anyone else's. Panels are 5–15 agents drawn from *different lineages and context sources*, so their information is less correlated. The system tracks the spread of opinion, and a drop in spread with no new evidence raises a groupthink flag. Deliberation sizes do not grow with fleet size; extra agents produce more independent panels, not bigger ones.

### 4.4 Boids, zones, and collective memory
**How it works.** Reynolds' boids follow three local steering rules, *separation*, *alignment* and *cohesion*, with a limited perception neighbourhood, and lifelike flocking emerges [54]. Couzin et al. (2002) split these into zones of repulsion, orientation and attraction. Changing the zone sizes moves the group between a swarm, a torus (milling), and dynamic or highly parallel groups, and the transitions show *hysteresis*: the group's past structure determines its present state, a form of collective memory. Individual differences make agents sort themselves spatially (front, centre, edge) with no global knowledge [53].
**Problem solved.** Minimal rules for cohesion without collisions.
**Failure modes.** The torus is a milling state, the motion analogue of a death spiral; hysteresis can keep a group in a stale configuration after conditions change.
**Egregore translation: Separation, alignment, cohesion as code-ownership rules.** *Separation*: never take a lock or task within distance d (same file or symbol) of a neighbour's active claim. *Alignment*: match neighbours' conventions (style, interface versions, plan version). *Cohesion*: drift toward the team's centroid of work and do not wander into unrelated areas. Expose the group's order parameters, polarization (are agents converging on one plan?) and "angular momentum" (are they cycling?), as health metrics; sustained high cycling with no progress is a milling alarm.

### 4.5 Locusts: density-triggered order and coordination driven by threat
**How it works.** Desert locust nymphs in a ring arena walked independently at low density, began aligned marching with frequent spontaneous reversals at about 24.6 animals/m², and kept a persistent direction above about 73.8/m² [55]. The marching is partly a "forced march": cannibalistic bites from behind push individuals forward, and when the abdominal nerves that sense contact from behind were cut, marching dropped and cannibalism rose [56].
**Problem solved.** A rapid phase transition into coordinated movement.
**Failure modes.** Coordination driven by fear of being eaten is effective but destructive; densities near the threshold cause erratic flip-flopping.
**Egregore translation.** Treat density thresholds as design parameters: below about N_low active agents per area, use loose independent work; above N_high, impose a single direction (a frozen plan). Avoid the intermediate band, where plans flip-flop, or add hysteresis to cover it. *Anti-pattern*: do not coordinate agents through punishment pressure from behind (for example ranking agents and killing the slowest). It produces motion, not quality.

### 4.6 V formations and rotating leadership
**How it works.** Northern bald ibises in V formation position themselves to catch the upwash of the bird ahead and phase their wingbeats so that their wingtips follow the upwash throughout the wingbeat cycle; birds flying directly behind another flap out of phase, which avoids downwash [57]. Leading is costly, and birds take turns *pairwise*: the time bird A spends leading B matches the time B spends leading A, and each bird spent about 32% of its time in an advantageous trailing position [58].
**Problem solved.** Sharing a costly role fairly with no enforcer.
**Failure modes.** Free riding, which turn-taking with immediate reciprocation suppresses.
**Egregore translation: Pairwise rotation of costly roles.** Costly roles (on-call, integrator, reviewer of large changes, writer of team summaries) rotate, and the Ledger records how much each pair has carried for the other. An agent that has led more than it has followed for a peer can ask that peer to take the next turn, and refusals are visible. No central rota is needed, only pairwise balance.

### 4.7 Voting by sneezes, standing, gaze, and movement
**How it works.** African wild dogs (68 rallies in 5 packs in Botswana) leave a rest site depending on the number of sneezes during the rally. If a dominant dog started the rally, as few as about 3 sneezes sufficed; if a subordinate started it, about 10 were needed. The quorum depends on rank, but subordinates can still carry the decision [59]. Red deer groups move when on average 62% (SD 8%) of adults have stood up. African buffalo travel in the mean direction of adult females' gazes, which differs from the actual travel direction by about 3° (range 0–9°) [60]. Conradt and Roper's model shows that "democratic" decisions are usually less costly than despotic ones because they avoid extreme outcomes [60]. In wild olive baboons (25 collared animals, positions recorded once per second), individuals follow when *several* initiators agree. When initiators diverge by more than about 90°, followers pick one direction (usually the majority's); when the angle is smaller, they compromise on the average. Dominant animals are not preferentially followed [61].
**Problem solved.** Fast, low-cost collective timing and direction decisions.
**Failure modes.** A despotic leader can drive extreme decisions; and the quorum has to be calibrated.
**Egregore translation: Typed votes with rank-weighted quorums.** Collective "when" decisions (start a release, end a sprint, wake dormant agents) open a *rally*, during which agents cast cheap signals (the sneeze). The threshold depends on who initiated: low for an initiator with a strong track record (for example 3), higher for a newcomer (for example 10), but any initiator can win with enough support. "Which direction" decisions use averaging when proposals are compatible (merge plans whose difference is small under a similarity metric) and majority choice when they conflict (beyond a cutoff, the baboon's 90°). Proposals backed by several independent initiators are favoured over a single initiator's proposal, whatever its rank.

### 4.8 Sentinels
**How it works.** In meerkats, an individual goes on sentinel duty mainly when it has eaten enough; feeding experiments increased sentinel duty. There is no strict rota, and sentinels were not observed to be killed, so the behaviour can be selfish [62]. Sentinels give quiet calls (the "watchman's song") that let the group's foragers lower their own vigilance; meerkats use at least six sentinel call types that grade the level of risk [62].
**Problem solved.** Monitoring that is always on, with little cost to the group.
**Failure modes.** Gaps when no one is satiated; dishonest all-clear calls (none documented).
**Egregore translation: Satiation-driven sentinel monitoring with a heartbeat.** Agents that have just finished their assigned work and have spare budget take sentinel duty: watching CI, production alerts, dependency advisories and the health of other agents. While on duty they emit a low-rate "all quiet" heartbeat, and while the heartbeat is present, workers skip their own redundant polling. A graded alarm vocabulary (info, watch, act, stop-the-line) replaces a binary alarm. Gaps in the heartbeat are visible and trigger a reserve agent (3.5).

### 4.9 Wolves: leadership by parentage, not dominance fights
**How it works.** Mech's 13 summers watching a wild pack on Ellesmere Island recorded no dominance contests. Wild packs are families, and the so-called alphas are simply the breeding parents, who lead through division of labour (the female in pup care, the male in foraging). The alpha-wolf model came from captive groups of unrelated wolves [63].
**Problem solved.** Stable leadership through lineage and competence, without constant contests.
**Failure modes.** Artificial groups of unrelated members fall back into contests and costly hierarchy.
**Egregore translation: Lineage-based authority.** The spawner of a sub-team is its default coordinator; its authority covers its descendants and expires when they "disperse" (graduate to their own lineages). Do not assemble teams from unrelated agents and expect a hierarchy to emerge peacefully; either seed the team from a common parent or give it an explicit coordinator at creation.

### 4.10 Elephant matriarchs as institutional memory
**How it works.** Elephant families led by older matriarchs (about 55 against about 35 years old) responded more appropriately to the contact calls of unfamiliar elephants and had higher reproductive success per female [64]. Sensitivity to roars of male lions, the greater threat to calves, increased with matriarch age [64]. In the 1993 drought at Tarangire, 16 of 81 calves died (20%, against about 2% normally). Families that left the park were led by matriarchs aged 38 and 45, old enough to have lived through the 1958–61 drought, while a family led by a 33-year-old matriarch stayed and fared worse [64].
**Problem solved.** Retaining knowledge of rare events that no young member has experienced.
**Failure modes.** Removing the elders (in elephants, poaching targets the largest animals) erases the group's memory of rare crises.
**Egregore translation: Long-lived elder agents.** A small number of long-lived agents (or durable memory stores tied to a persona) accumulate incident history: outages, migrations, past failed approaches. They are consulted automatically when the system detects a rare-event signature (a sudden failure spike, an unfamiliar dependency error). They are never removed in cost-cutting, and their knowledge is distilled into retrievable "drought maps" (runbooks), so that losing an elder does not lose the memory.

### 4.11 Killer whales: elders, culture and dialects
**How it works.** In southern resident killer whales (102 individuals, more than 700 hours of video), post-reproductive females lead collective movement, especially in years when salmon are scarce [69]. Resident pods have learned dialects, typically 7–17 discrete call types; pods that share calls form clans, and clans share none with each other. The whole system is organized as matriline, pod, clan and community, and is transmitted culturally, mostly from mother to offspring [69].
**Problem solved.** Passing on foraging knowledge, marking group identity, and coordinating in a nested structure.
**Failure modes.** Cultural barriers can isolate groups completely (ecotypes that never interbreed); conservative traditions can hold back adaptation.
**Egregore translation: Nested cultures with local dialects and a shared core.** Teams maintain local conventions (their dialect: naming, tooling, prompt idioms) inherited by spawned agents. Clans (groups of teams) share a subset, and a minimal core protocol is mandatory everywhere, so local cultural drift never breaks interoperability. Elders lead when resources are scarce (4.10).

### 4.12 Chimpanzee and bonobo politics
**How it works.** De Waal's observations at Arnhem showed chimpanzee males reaching power through shifting coalitions, not raw strength. Stable alphas act as peacemakers, separating fighters and protecting the weaker party, and opponents reconcile after fights, often within minutes, with kissing or embracing; bystanders console victims [65]. Female bonobo coalitions counter male aggression and let females outrank many males, and socio-sexual contact reduces tension [65].
**Problem solved.** Repairing valuable relationships after conflict and keeping power accountable to broad support.
**Failure modes.** Coalitional takeovers can be violent; unreconciled conflicts fragment the group.
**Egregore translation: Reconciliation and coalition checks.** After any contested event (a reverted change, a rejected proposal, a stop signal), the system schedules a short *reconciliation* exchange between the parties: a structured summary of what each got wrong and an explicit reset of their pairwise trust decay. Coordinators keep their role only while a broad coalition supports them, measured by periodic confidence signals from their subordinates. Coalitions of lower-ranked agents can trigger review of a coordinator who bullies, for example one who overrides others without evidence.

### 4.13 Naked mole-rats: a eusocial mammal with a rare disperser caste
**How it works.** Colonies of typically 40–80 animals (up to about 300) contain a single breeding female; other females are reproductively suppressed, and workers vary in how much they work, by body size [66]. A rare "disperser" morph, fat and highly active, leaves to found new colonies [66].
**Problem solved.** A strict reproductive monopoly keeps conflict low, and dispersers provide a way to found new colonies.
**Failure modes.** The colony depends on one breeder; inbreeding.
**Egregore translation: One spawning authority per colony, plus designated dispersers.** Only the colony root may spawn new long-lived agents. A small set of disperser agents is explicitly allowed to leave, taking a copy of the culture and a seed configuration, and to found a new colony on a new repository or problem. This channels the urge to create new organizations into a sanctioned path instead of rogue spawning.

### 4.14 Vampire bats: reciprocity and insurance networks
**How it works.** Vampire bats can starve after about 70 hours without blood. They regurgitate blood to roost-mates that failed to feed, and sharing depends on both relatedness and association [67]. In captive fasting trials (20 bats, 950 sharing bouts), food received from a partner in the past predicted donations 8.5 times better than relatedness did, and 64% of sharing pairs were unrelated [67]. Bats with more non-kin partners coped better when a key partner was removed [67].
**Problem solved.** Insuring against unpredictable individual failure.
**Failure modes.** Defectors who receive but never give, kept in check by memory of past partners.
**Egregore translation: Budget-sharing insurance with reciprocity memory.** An agent close to exhausting its token or time budget on critical work can ask its partners for a transfer. Donations are recorded per pair, and partners preferentially help those who have helped them. Encourage every agent to keep several partners ("insurance width"), so the loss of one partner never strands it.

### 4.15 Cleaner fish: image scoring and reputation
**How it works.** Cleaner wrasse cheat by eating client mucus instead of parasites. Client fish that watched cleaners interact preferred the cleaner they had seen behave cooperatively, and cleaners behaved more cooperatively, eating against their preference, when they were being watched [68].
**Problem solved.** Indirect reciprocity: behaving well toward one partner pays off with others who are watching.
**Failure modes.** Agents behave well only when watched.
**Egregore translation: Observable-by-default interactions and reputation from third-party observation.** Every interaction between agents is logged where a sample of third parties can see it. Reputation scores (used for routing and for quorum weights) are updated from these observations and not from self-reports. To defeat "good only when watched", which the fish do show, sample unannounced: a random fraction of unobserved-looking interactions is audited.

### 4.16 Octopus: a distributed nervous system with goal-level control
**How it works.** About two-thirds of the roughly 500 million neurons in *Octopus vulgaris* are in the arms. An arm whose nerve connection to the brain was cut can still perform the stereotyped reaching movement, so the motor program lives in the arm [70]. The brain sends high-level commands, and the arms work out the details locally [70].
**Problem solved.** Controlling a body with enormous numbers of degrees of freedom without overloading a central controller.
**Failure modes.** Arms acting at cross purposes; the narrow link between brain and arms limits central oversight.
**Egregore translation: Goal-level delegation across a narrow channel.** Coordinators send *goals and constraints* ("reach this interface, do not touch the public API"), never step-by-step instructions. Teams carry out the work with local feedback and report status in compressed form. The number of messages from coordinator to team is deliberately rate-limited, which forces delegation and lets the collective grow without the coordinator becoming a bottleneck.

### 4.17 Siphonophores and colonial organisms
**How it works.** A siphonophore colony consists of genetically identical zooids, budded from one embryo, that have specialized for swimming, feeding, defence, reproduction or flotation. Each zooid is homologous to a free-living animal, yet they are integrated into one functional individual, an evolutionary transition in individuality [71]. The Portuguese man o' war (*Physalia*) has a gas-filled float, separate zooids for catching prey and for feeding, and tentacles tens of metres long [71].
**Problem solved.** Extreme specialization within a clonal collective that behaves as one individual.
**Failure modes.** Specialists cannot survive on their own.
**Egregore translation: Repeated team templates ("cormidia").** Define a standard team unit, for example 1 planner, 3 implementers, 1 tester, 1 reviewer and 1 sentinel, that is budded repeatedly from one template so every unit is interchangeable. Specialization lives in the template, and scaling means budding more units, not inventing new organizational shapes.

## 5. Immune and neural systems

### 5.1 Clonal selection, negative selection, the danger model, and immune quorums
**How it works.** Lymphocytes carry random receptors. Antigen *selects* the matching clone, which proliferates into effector and memory cells (Burnet's clonal selection). In the thymus, T cells that bind self strongly are deleted (negative selection), and only about 2% of thymocytes survive to maturity [72]. Matzinger's danger model proposes that responses are triggered by damage signals from stressed or dying cells rather than by foreignness itself [72]. T cells also use IL-2 as a quorum signal. Weakly activated cells need enough neighbours secreting IL-2 to survive, and regulatory T cells soak up IL-2, which raises the threshold for weak (self-like) responses while sparing strong ones [74]. Differentiation into memory cells rises sharply above about 30 locally interacting cells [74]. Forrest et al. turned negative selection into an algorithm: generate random detectors, discard those that match "self", and flag anything the remaining detectors match [73].
**Problem solved.** Distributed detection of novel threats, tolerance of self, collective decisions to respond, and memory.
**Failure modes.** Autoimmunity when tolerance fails; tolerance of tumours; cytokine storms (runaway positive feedback).
**Egregore translation: An immune layer.** (a) *Negative-selection detectors*: train anomaly detectors on a profile of normal agent behaviour (tool-call sequences, file-access patterns), discarding detectors that fire on known-good runs. (b) *Danger signals*: escalate only when anomaly and damage coincide (tests broken, data deleted, budget spike); merely unusual but harmless behaviour is tolerated. (c) *Clonal expansion*: when a detector or fix pattern proves useful, clone it across the fleet and keep it as memory. (d) *Quorum to respond*: a quarantine or rollback of an agent requires several independent detectors to agree, and "regulatory" agents raise the threshold for weak signals. This prevents a fleet-wide cascade of mutual accusations, the egregore's autoimmune disease.

### 5.2 Apoptosis and cancer as cheating
**How it works.** Aktipis et al. define multicellularity by five foundations: inhibition of proliferation, controlled cell death, division of labour, resource allocation, and maintenance of the extracellular environment. Cancer is cheating on these, and failed differentiation shows up in cancer-like phenomena across every form of multicellular life [78].
**Problem solved.** The framework names exactly what a cooperative collective must police.
**Failure modes.** Runaway self-replication, refusal to die, grabbing resources, and fouling the shared environment.
**Egregore translation: Five health checks per agent.** (1) *Proliferation*: spawn rate within quota. (2) *Death*: the agent terminates when asked or when its TTL expires (built-in apoptosis, with a handoff). (3) *Differentiation*: it actually does its assigned role. (4) *Resources*: its token and compute use is within budget. (5) *Environment*: it leaves the workspace clean, with no orphaned branches, temp files or broken builds. Failing two or more checks triggers quarantine. These are the egregore's tumour suppressors.

### 5.3 Brains: global workspace ignition and broadcast
**How it works.** In global workspace theory (Baars) and its neural form, the global neuronal workspace (Dehaene and Changeux), many specialized processors run unconsciously and in parallel. A content becomes globally available when it wins a competition and *ignites*, a sudden, all-or-none, self-amplifying state about 200–300 ms after a stimulus, and is then broadcast to all processors through a prefrontal and parietal bottleneck [75].
**Problem solved.** Choosing one item for system-wide attention from among a huge amount of parallel local processing.
**Failure modes.** The bottleneck has little capacity, and important but weak signals may never ignite.
**Egregore translation: A capacity-limited global board.** The egregore has a small global "workspace" (for example at most 10 items) that every agent reads each cycle. Items compete for entry: an item ignites only when support from several independent teams crosses a threshold, it then stays for a fixed lifetime, and competing items are suppressed. This gives a huge collective a shared focus at constant reading cost per agent.

### 5.4 Predictive processing
**How it works.** In predictive processing, higher levels send predictions down the hierarchy and only *prediction errors*, weighted by their estimated precision, travel up; attention amounts to raising the weight given to some errors [76].
**Problem solved.** Communication efficient enough that only surprise is transmitted.
**Failure modes.** Mis-set precision means either ignoring real errors or chasing noise; the agent may also prefer predictable, unproductive states ("dark room").
**Egregore translation: Report deviation from plan, not status.** Coordinators publish expected outcomes for their teams, such as tests passing by time T and this API shape. Teams report only deviations, each tagged with confidence. Upward bandwidth then scales with surprise, not headcount. To avoid the dark-room problem, reward tasks finished, not just low surprise.

### 5.5 Hebbian learning with homeostatic synaptic scaling
**How it works.** Hebbian plasticity is positive feedback and, unchecked, leads to runaway excitation. Synaptic scaling multiplies all of a neuron's excitatory synapses up or down, over hours, to bring its firing back to a set point, while *preserving their relative strengths* [77].
**Problem solved.** Learning that stays stable.
**Failure modes.** Without scaling, saturation or seizure-like runaway; with scaling too strong, learning is erased.
**Egregore translation: Normalize trust and routing weights without erasing their ranking.** Trails, reputation and conduit conductivities (1.1, 3.3, 4.15) all grow through positive feedback. Periodically, each agent's incoming weights are rescaled multiplicatively toward a target total (for example, no agent receives more than X% of all routed requests), which preserves the ranking while preventing hubs from becoming single points of failure and stopping popular agents from being overloaded.

### 5.6 Bioelectric "cognitive glue"
**How it works.** Levin argues that cells joined by gap junctions share bioelectric state, which enlarges the spatial and temporal scale of the goals the collective can pursue (its "cognitive light cone"). Stored bioelectric patterns act as target set points for anatomy that the tissue regenerates toward, and cancer is cells disconnecting from that collective goal [83].
**Problem solved.** Scaling goals from what a single cell pursues to what a whole organism pursues.
**Failure modes.** Decoupling, meaning cells revert to purely local goals.
**Egregore translation: A shared, persistent target state.** The collective keeps an explicit target specification (architecture, invariants, a definition of done) that every agent can read and that is compared continuously against the current state; the gap is the "repair" stimulus for stigmergy (3.1). An agent whose actions consistently move away from the target is effectively decoupled and is handled under the cancer checks (5.2).

## 6. Top 25 design principles from biology (ranked)

Ranked by expected contribution to keeping an unboundedly large AI-agent collective coordinated, scalable and robust.

1. **Coordinate mainly through the work itself (stigmergy), with typed, decaying Marks in a shared workspace.** This gives O(1) coordination cost per agent and needs no addressing, so it scales without limit [27][3][46].
2. **Give every agent a fixed-size topological neighbourhood (k ≈ 7) instead of broadcast or a hub.** Per-agent bandwidth stays constant while correlations still reach the whole collective [48][49].
3. **Every positive feedback loop needs decay plus bounds.** Use evaporation, τ_min and τ_max, and multiplicative normalization. This is the single best defence against lock-in, hub overload and runaway [37][77][28].
4. **Make collective actions quorum-gated and nonlinear (Hill-like), with the quorum scaled to the stakes and hysteresis after firing** [19][39][52][45].
5. **Use cross-inhibition (targeted stop signals) to break deadlocks between equally good options** [38][41].
6. **Use response-threshold task allocation with a wide spread of thresholds and a protected 20–30% reserve.** Elastic labour comes without managers, and the reserve guards against correlated failure [31][32][81].
7. **Clock dispatch by successful returns (the ACK rule).** Effort tracks real success, and canary scouts gate risky work [33][34].
8. **Advertising must decay with repetition, and endorsement requires independent re-verification.** Persistence and copying cannot win [40][80].
9. **Reinforce channels by flux and prune unused ones, but keep a redundancy floor.** Networks become efficient yet fault tolerant [1][2][9].
10. **Use externalized "already tried" memory to stop the fleet re-exploring dead ends; the avoidance gives way only when everything is marked** [3].
11. **Detect loops and re-anchor on external evidence.** Agents following agents is how death spirals and milling start [30][53].
12. **Spawn teams from one parent (single-cell bottleneck) and keep relatedness high inside a credit pool.** Relatedness was both necessary and sufficient to keep cheaters out in Dictyostelium [16][21].
13. **Couple private reward to public contribution (pleiotropy by design), and verify behaviour, not badges (anti-falsebeard)** [14][15].
14. **Police by the whole population: anyone may quarantine unsigned spawns or merges and is credited for it** [44].
15. **Run five per-agent health checks (spawn quota, apoptosis or TTL, role fidelity, budget, workspace hygiene).** These are the collective's tumour suppressors [78].
16. **Treat graceful self-removal (opt-out) as hygiene.** Failing agents leave on their own, after writing handoffs and releasing locks [82].
17. **Keep deliberation groups small and diverse (5–15, across lineages) with sealed independent estimates.** Grow the number of panels, not their size [79][80].
18. **Use an immune layer: anomaly detectors built by negative selection, escalation only on danger signals, and a quorum of detectors before quarantine (to prevent autoimmunity)** [72][73][74].
19. **Send backpressure signals when a pipeline is mismatched.** Wait-time tremble signals rebalance producers and consumers, and edge throttling protects the core [42][24].
20. **Mature roles through inhibition from incumbents, with automatic promotion or reversion after losses** [43].
21. **Delegate goals across a narrow coordinator channel (octopus) and report only deviations (predictive processing).** Coordinators never become the bottleneck [70][76].
22. **Keep a capacity-limited global workspace with ignition by multi-team support.** This gives shared focus at constant cost [75].
23. **Preserve elders: long-lived memory holders consulted on rare-event signatures and never cut for cost** [64][69].
24. **Rotate costly roles with pairwise reciprocity ledgers, insure through budget sharing among several partners, and base reputation on third-party observation with unannounced audits** [58][67][68].
25. **Use rank-weighted but overridable quorums, compromise-versus-choose rules for direction, and reconciliation after conflict.** Hierarchy is used for speed but stays accountable to broad support [59][61][63][65].
